Sensor-Actuated Control for an Eight-Movement Intersection

A design study for a four-leg intersection with separately signalled straight and left-turn movements, two presence sensors per movement, a demand-responsive controller that switches only the signals that must change, and a fair fixed-time baseline to compare it against.

Source value taken from a cited standard, manual or paper Derived computed by us from sourced formulas Assumption our own engineering choice, with reasoning

01Intersection model

The site is an isolated four-leg “+” intersection with right-hand traffic. Each approach has two lanes entering the junction: an inner left-turn lane and an outer straight lane. Each lane has its own signal head and two virtual point sensors, D1 near the stop line and D2 upstream. Right turns are outside the system, so they are neither simulated nor signalled.

Movement names follow the approach the vehicle comes from. NS is a vehicle entering from the North leg and driving straight through to the South exit. NL enters from the North and turns left, which for a southbound driver means leaving by the East exit.

02Assumptions

These choices define the scope. Everything else in the study follows from them, so they should be copied into the report's method chapter.

  1. Isolated intersection. No neighbouring signals, so arrivals are random rather than in platoons and no coordination or offsets are needed. The FHWA Signal Timing Manual identifies fully actuated control as best suited to exactly this case.
  2. Passenger cars only. This allows the HCM base saturation flow without heavy-vehicle adjustment.
  3. Protected left turns only. Each left lane has its own signal, so a left turn moves only on its own green arrow and never filters through opposing traffic.
  4. Merging counts as a conflict. Two movements that end in the same exit leg are never green together, even though the exit has two lanes. This is conservative and matches standard phasing practice.
  5. No pedestrians, bicycles, right turns or right-turn-on-red. These are out of scope for the brief. Pedestrian timing would add minimum-green constraints (FHWA Eq. 5-1).
  6. All drivers comply with signals and never block the box.
  7. The controller sees only sensors. The smart controller may read only the 16 sensor states and its own timers. It must never read the simulator's true queue lengths, or the comparison is unfair to real hardware. This rule matters more than any single parameter.
  8. One lane per movement, so lane choice and lane changing are not modelled. A vehicle's movement is fixed when it is generated.

This is the same structure as the standard NEMA dual-ring, eight-phase controller: one ring holds a left and the opposing straight in sequence, the barrier separates the two axes, and any phase from ring 1 may run with any phase from ring 2 on the same side of the barrier. Your example in the brief is consistent with it: SS+SL can become SS+NS, because NS is compatible with SS but conflicts with SL.

A single movement on its own (for example NS alone) is always allowed. The controller only turns on members of a group that actually have demand.

04Sensor model

Your prototype uses IR break-beam or reflective sensors. These are point detectors: they report whether a vehicle body is in front of them at this instant. The model below is built from that property rather than from loop-detector conventions.

Placement

We place each sensor at the centre of a queue “slot”, so that a stopped vehicle, and not the gap between two stopped vehicles, sits in front of it. With a vehicle length of 4.5 m and a standstill gap of 3.0 m, the queue spacing is 7.5 m. That is close to the 25 ft (7.6 m) average spacing the FHWA manual uses to convert detector distance into queued vehicles Source.

SensorDistance before stop lineCovered byPurpose
D1 stop-line sensor2.0 mQueued vehicle #1 (occupies 0.5–5.0 m)Detects a waiting vehicle; extends green while the queue discharges over it
D2 queue / advance sensor32.0 mQueued vehicle #5 (occupies 30.5–35.0 m)Detects a queue of 5 or more; gives ~2.3 s early warning of an approaching car at 50 km/h

For the physical prototype, scale the same idea: put D1 under the first model car and D2 under the fifth car of a standing queue.

Recommended interpretation

The brief suggests “no sensor = no demand, D1 = short queue, both = long queue”. That is roughly right, but a point sensor gives two false readings the naive rule does not handle. A car driving past D2 triggers it briefly even when there is no queue. A queue of three cars triggers D1 but not D2, which the naive rule reads correctly, yet a single car still approaching triggers D2 alone. We therefore interpret the sensors differently depending on the signal state.

Signal stateSensor eventController meaning
RED (movement waiting)Any pulse on D1 or D2Call registered and latched until the movement is served (“locking memory”). Waiting timer t_call starts.
D1 occupied, D2 notLevel 1: 1–4 vehicles queued. Estimated queue Q̂ = 2.
D2 occupied continuously ≥ 2.0 sLevel 2: queue reaches the 5th slot, so ≥ 5 vehicles. Q̂ = 6. Latched until green. The 2.0 s filter rejects cars merely driving past, which cover a point sensor for about 0.3–0.6 s.
GREEN (movement served)Any occupancy on D1 or D2Resets the passage timer. The green is extended while vehicles keep arriving.
Neither sensor occupied for the passage time (2.5 s)Gap-out: the queue has cleared and arrivals are sparse, so the movement may be released.
At the moment of green onsetLevel 2 latchedUse the extended initial minimum green (12 s) so the known ≥ 5-vehicle queue is cleared (see §6).

Why this is defensible. It is how actuated controllers already use detectors. A call is placed on red, the green is extended by actuations within a passage time, and the phase gaps out on a long enough headway (FHWA Signal Timing Manual §5.4.2). Using an upstream detector to size the initial green is the “variable initial” feature (§5.5.2). The only addition is the two-level queue estimate, which is our own Assumption and is stated as such.

Optional sensor extensions (for robustness experiments)

  • Count-based estimate: count D2 pulses in and D1 pulses out to estimate the queue. It is more precise, but a single missed pulse corrupts the count until reset. Treat it as an extension, not the default.
  • Sensor faults: a missed-detection probability (for example 5%), a stuck-on D1 and a stuck-off D2. A stuck-on detector should cause max-outs on that movement, never a lock-up. This is the standard fail-safe behaviour described in the FHWA manual.

05Vehicle movement model

We recommend a queue-server model with spatial positions. Stop-line departures follow the HCM saturation-headway model, which gives defensible capacity. Positions along the lane exist so that sensors can be evaluated and cars can be drawn.

Arrival

  • Each movement has its own arrival stream. Headways are shifted exponential: h = 1.5 s + Exp(mean = 3600/q − 1.5), where q is in veh/h. This is Poisson-like random arrival, as expected at an isolated intersection, but no two cars in a lane arrive closer than 1.5 s Assumption.
  • Vehicles appear 150 m upstream at the free speed of 50 km/h (13.9 m/s) Assumption.
  • Arrival lists are generated before the run from a seed and saved to CSV. Both controllers read the same file, which is how “same vehicle arrivals” is guaranteed.

Approach and queueing

  • A car drives at free speed until it reaches the back of the queue, then stops in the next 7.5 m slot. Use a 3 m/s² deceleration if you want smooth animation. The statistics do not depend on it.
  • If the signal is green and the car's lane is clear, it passes the stop line without stopping.

Discharge (the part that makes timing realistic)

For a queue waiting at the start of green at time t_g, the n-th vehicle crosses the stop line at:

t_n = t_g + l1 + n · h        l1 = 2.0 s start-up lost time,  h = 1.9 s (straight) / 2.0 s (left)
  • The first car crosses about 3.9 s after green, the 5th about 11.5 s after, and the 10th about 21 s after. A queue therefore needs real time to discharge, which is the behaviour you asked for.
  • For any vehicle: departure ≥ max(own arrival at stop line, previous departure + h, t_g + l1 + h). This handles cars that join a moving queue.
  • Yellow: a vehicle may still cross during the first 2.0 s of yellow, the HCM “extension of effective green” Source. After that it stops.
  • After crossing, the car follows its path through the box (straight: 20 m, left: ≈ 25 m arc) at about 8–10 m/s. This is visual only, and the all-red interval is sized for it.

Because l1 = e = 2.0 s, the effective green of every phase equals its displayed green. That keeps the report's capacity arithmetic simple: capacity = s × G / C.

06Timing research

Each value below has a source, or is derived from a sourced formula with our geometry. The consolidated list is in §14.

Saturation flow and discharge headway

HCM base saturation flow is 1,900 pc/h/ln, with a default start-up lost time of 2.0 s and 2.0 s of yellow used by drivers Source (HCM defaults, as documented by McTrans). 1,900 veh/h is a headway of 3600/1900 = 1.89 s. For protected lefts we apply the HCM left-turn factor of about 0.95, giving ≈ 1,800 veh/h, or 2.0 s Derived. The HCM allows 1,750 pc/h/ln for areas under 250,000 people. That can be used as a sensitivity test.

Yellow change interval

The MUTCD requires a yellow after every green and advises roughly 3 to 6 s, with longer values on faster approaches Source. The ITE kinematic formula (FHWA Eq. 5-2, first term) is Y = t + v / (2a) with t = 1.0 s and a = 10 ft/s² (3.05 m/s²) Source.

  • Straight, v = 50 km/h (13.9 m/s): Y = 1 + 13.9/6.1 = 3.28 s → 3.5 s (rounded up; FHWA Table 5-7 gives 3.2 s at 30 mph and 3.6 s at 35 mph) Derived.
  • Left, turning speed 30 km/h (8.3 m/s): Y = 1 + 8.3/6.1 = 2.4 s → 3.0 s, the MUTCD minimum Derived.

Red clearance (all-red)

The second term of Eq. 5-2 is R = (W + L) / v, the time to clear the junction. FHWA cites a 20 ft (6 m) vehicle length, the MUTCD says red clearance should not exceed 6 s, and a practitioner survey reports common values of 0.5–2.0 s Source.

  • Straight: (20 + 6) / 13.9 = 1.9 s → 2.0 s Derived.
  • Left: (25 + 6) / 8.3 = 3.7 s. Some agencies subtract 1.0 s for the reaction time of the next driver (FHWA §5.3.2), giving 2.7 s → 3.0 s Derived.

Change periods are therefore 5.5 s for straights and 6.0 s for lefts. In a partial transition, only the terminating movement's yellow and all-red must finish before a conflicting movement starts. Movements that stay green are not interrupted.

Minimum green

FHWA Table 5-3 gives typical minimum greens for driver expectancy: through movements on a minor arterial 4–10 s, on a major arterial at ≤ 40 mph 7–15 s, and left turns 2–5 s Source.

  • Straight: 7 s. This is within the range, and our discharge model clears about 3 vehicles (2 + 3 × 1.9 = 7.7 s) before extension takes over.
  • Left: 5 s, the top of the range, so at least 1–2 cars get through.
  • Extended initial when D2 is latched: 12 s for both. Five vehicles need 2 + 5 × 1.9 ≈ 11.5 s. The FHWA queue-clearance rule Gq = 3 + 2n, with n = 32 m / 7.6 m ≈ 4, gives 11 s, and Table 5-4 lists 11–13 s for detectors 76–125 ft upstream Derived.

Passage time (gap time)

FHWA Eq. 5-5 for presence detection is PT = MAH − (Lv + Ld) / v_a. The recommended maximum allowable headway is MAH = 3.0 s without gap reduction, the average speed is v_a = 0.88 × v85, and the efficient range is about 1–4 s Source.

For an IR point sensor, Ld ≈ 0. With Lv = 6 m and v_a = 0.88 × 13.9 = 12.2 m/s, PT = 3.0 − 0.49 = 2.5 s Derived. FHWA Table 5-10 gives 2.3 s for a 6 ft zone at 30 mph, which agrees. During queue discharge, cars pass D1 at about 1.9 s headway with about 1 s gaps, so the green is held until the queue is gone.

Maximum green

FHWA Table 5-5 gives example maxima: through on a minor arterial 30–50 s, on a major arterial at ≤ 40 mph 40–60 s, and left turns 15–30 s. The max-green timer starts only when a conflicting call exists Source.

  • Straight: 45 s. Left: 30 s. As a check, FHWA Table 5-6 (85th–95th percentile queue clearance) gives 43 s for 500 veh/h/ln at a 100 s cycle.
  • These caps also bound starvation (§9). A sensitivity run at 60/40 s is recommended, because heavy scenarios may max out.

07Smart control algorithm

The controller is a movement-based, fully actuated controller with free phase selection and partial transitions. It works like a standard actuated controller in that each movement has its own minimum green, passage timer, max-out, yellow and all-red. It differs from a fixed ring sequence in three ways:

  1. The next group is chosen by a priority score that combines estimated queue and waiting time, not a fixed order.
  2. Only the movements that conflict with the next target are stopped. Compatible greens keep running.
  3. Only movements that have a call are turned green, so a single signal can be open alone.

Priority score

P(m) = Q̂(m) + wait(m) / τ        Q̂ = 2 (level 1) or 6 (level 2);  wait = now − t_call(m);  τ = 10 s
S(G) = Σ P(m) over members of group G that have a call
     + Q̂_green = 2 for members of G that are green and not yet gapped out

τ = 10 s means every 10 s of waiting counts as one extra queued car. A lone car waiting 40 s (P = 2 + 4 = 6) ties with a fresh long queue, and after 60 s it wins. Both τ and the Q̂ levels are Assumptions, and we recommend a sensitivity run on τ.

The closest published idea is max-pressure control (Varaiya, 2013), which selects the phase with the largest weighted queue and has proven throughput-optimal properties. Our score is a simplified, sensor-limited version that adds waiting time for fairness. Cite max-pressure as related work, not as what we implement.

Rules that prevent rapid switching

  • No preemption of a working green. A green movement can be released only when it is releasable: its minimum green has elapsed and it has gapped out, maxed out, or conflicts with a starvation hold.
  • Gap-out uses the passage timer, not the instantaneous sensor state. One empty sensor reading never ends a green.
  • Commitment. Once a yellow starts, the target is locked until its greens have started. The decision is not re-evaluated mid-transition.
  • Target protection. While a higher-priority target is waiting, the controller will not start a new green that conflicts with that target, even when the new green is compatible with what is running now. Otherwise a stream of compatible additions could delay the target forever.
  • Timers survive partial transitions. If SS stays green while SL→NS changes around it, SS keeps its original green-start time and max-green timer, so it cannot stay green forever by “riding” changes.
  • Rest in green. With no conflicting calls, the current greens stay on (standard NEMA behaviour). Rest-in-red is available as an ablation.

The brief's example, step by step

Pseudocode

# ---------- static model ----------
MOVES   = [NS, NL, SS, SL, ES, EL, WS, WL]
GROUPS  = [{NS,SS},{NL,SL},{NS,NL},{SS,SL},{ES,WS},{EL,WL},{ES,EL},{WS,WL}]
compatible(a,b) = (a == b) or any({a,b} <= G for G in GROUPS)
MIN_G = {straight: 7, left: 5};  MIN_G_EXT = 12;  MAX_G = {straight: 45, left: 30}
PT = 2.5;  Y = {straight: 3.5, left: 3.0};  AR = {straight: 2.0, left: 3.0}
TAU = 10;  W_MAX = 90;  D2_FILTER = 2.0;  QHAT = {1: 2, 2: 6}

# ---------- per-movement state ----------
sig[m] in {RED, GREEN, YELLOW, ALLRED};  t_state[m]
call[m]=False; t_call[m]; level[m]=0; d2_on_since[m]
t_green[m]; min_g[m]; passage[m]; max_start[m]=None; gapped[m]
committed = None      # (stop_set, start_set) of a transition in progress

every step (dt = 0.1 s):
    read D1[m], D2[m] for all m
    advance_intervals()                 # GREEN>YELLOW>ALLRED>RED by fixed durations
    update_calls(); update_green_timers()
    if committed: finish_transition_if_clear(); return
    decide()

update_calls():
    for m in MOVES:
        if sig[m] != GREEN:
            if (D1[m] or D2[m]) and not call[m]: call[m]=True; t_call[m]=now
            if D2[m] held for >= D2_FILTER s: level[m]=2          # latched
            elif call[m] and level[m]==0:        level[m]=1

update_green_timers():
    for m with sig[m]==GREEN:
        passage[m] = 0 if (D1[m] or D2[m]) else passage[m]+dt
        gapped[m]  = passage[m] >= PT
        if max_start[m] is None and any(call[c] and not compatible(m,c) for c in MOVES):
            max_start[m] = now                                     # FHWA: max timer on conflicting call

releasable(m, hold):
    if now - t_green[m] < min_g[m]: return False
    maxed = max_start[m] and now - max_start[m] >= MAX_G[type(m)]
    return gapped[m] or maxed or (hold and not compatible(m, hold))

decide():
    calls = [m for m in MOVES if call[m]]
    if not calls: return                                         # rest in current greens
    hold   = argmax(wait) over calls with wait(m) >= W_MAX, else None
    greens = {m | sig[m]==GREEN};  clearing = {m | sig[m] in (YELLOW, ALLRED)}
    P      = {m: QHAT[level[m]] + (now - t_call[m]) / TAU for m in calls}
    cands  = [G for G in GROUPS if hold is None or hold in G]
    target = argmax over cands of score(G)                         # ties: keep greens, then fixed order

    # (a) add compatible calls without stopping anything
    for m in calls:
        if all(compatible(m,x) for x in greens | clearing) and all(compatible(m,t) for t in target):
            start_green(m)

    # (b) switch toward target only when every conflicting green is releasable
    to_stop  = {g for g in greens if g not in target}
    to_start = {m for m in target if call[m] and sig[m]==RED}
    if to_start and all(releasable(g, hold) for g in to_stop):
        for g in to_stop: set_yellow(g)                        # only the conflicting ones
        committed = (to_stop, to_start)

finish_transition_if_clear():
    stop, start = committed
    for m in start:
        if all(sig[c]==RED or compatible(m,c) for c in MOVES): start_green(m)
    if all(sig[m]==GREEN for m in start): committed = None

start_green(m):
    sig[m]=GREEN; t_green[m]=now; passage[m]=0; gapped[m]=False; max_start[m]=None
    min_g[m] = MIN_G_EXT if level[m]==2 else MIN_G[type(m)]
    call[m]=False; level[m]=0
    # after a max-out with cars still present, D1/D2 re-place a call on the next red

Invariant to assert in code every step: no two non-RED movements (GREEN, YELLOW or ALLRED) may conflict. Put this assertion in the simulator, not only in the controller. It is your safety proof in the report.

08Fixed-time baseline

The baseline is a conventional four-phase, leading dual-left plan, the most common textbook and field layout for protected lefts:

φA  NL + SL   →   φB  NS + SS   →   φC  EL + WL   →   φD  ES + WS   →  (repeat)

Each phase ends with its own yellow and all-red (5.5 s for straights, 6.0 s for lefts). The baseline uses the same vehicle model, arrival files and safety intervals as the smart controller. It never skips a phase and never reacts to sensors.

Green times from Webster's method

Webster's optimum cycle is C₀ = (1.5 L + 5) / (1 − Y). L is the total lost time and Y is the sum of critical flow ratios v/s for each phase. Green is then split in proportion to each phase's critical ratio Source. Here L = 2 × 5.5 + 2 × 6.0 = 23 s, with cycle bounds of 60–120 s and minimum greens of 7 s (straight) and 5 s (left) Assumption.

Validating the simulator with the baseline

Before comparing controllers, run FT-A on S1 and check the simulated average delay against Webster's delay formula for random arrivals: about 35 s per straight vehicle and 58 s per left-turn vehicle Derived. Agreement within roughly ±20% shows the arrival and discharge model is sound. Put this check in the report. Examiners value it.

09Fairness and starvation handling

Three mechanisms work together. Each can be switched off for an ablation study.

  1. Max green with a conflict-triggered timer Source: a green facing a conflicting call ends after at most 45 s (straight) or 30 s (left), even if cars keep coming. This alone stops one busy movement from holding the junction.
  2. Aging in the priority score Assumption: wait/τ grows without limit, so any waiting movement eventually outranks any fresh queue. This handles the “largest queue always wins” problem the brief warns about.
  3. Hard waiting limit W_MAX = 90 s Assumption: once any call has waited 90 s, it becomes a hold. The next target must contain it, no new green conflicting with it may start, and conflicting greens become releasable as soon as their minimum green is over.

Why 90 s: under the FT-A plan, a left-turn vehicle arriving just after its phase ends waits up to 89 − 8 = 81 s plus the start-up time. The 90 s limit therefore promises that the smart controller never makes anyone wait meaningfully longer than the worst case under fixed time.

Resulting bound Derived: once a hold exists, it is served within the largest remaining minimum green (≤ 12 s) plus one change period (≤ 6 s). The worst case is about W_MAX + 18 ≈ 108 s. Report the measured maximum wait against this bound.

Fairness measures (see §11): maximum and 95th-percentile delay, the number of vehicles waiting over 90 s, and Jain's fairness index over the eight movements' mean delays, J = (Σx)² / (n·Σx²). J = 1 means all movements are treated equally.

Why this set is fair

  • S1 and S7a are the “fixed time's home ground”: steady, symmetric traffic, where a tuned fixed plan is close to optimal. We expect small or no gains for the smart controller here, and the report should say so.
  • S7b is included to expose a limitation. When every movement is oversaturated, an actuated controller maxes out every phase and behaves almost like fixed time. Extra switching may even make it slightly worse.
  • S3, S4, S5 and S6 are where sensors should help: skipping empty phases, short cycles at night, reacting to bursts and shifts.
  • S9 tests whether split groups ({NS,NL}, {SS,SL}) and partial transitions help when opposing demands are unequal.
  • S10 directly tests the starvation guarantee.

Optional robustness runs: S1 with 5% missed detections, S1 with NS D1 stuck on, and a demand sweep scaling S1 volumes by 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.15 and 1.3.

Rules to keep the comparison honest

  • Freeze every smart-controller parameter before running experiments. Tune only on S1 with seeds 101–105, then run all scenarios on seeds 1–10. Tuning on the test scenarios is the easiest way to accidentally bias the study.
  • Use paired comparisons: the same seed and the same arrival file for every controller. Report the mean difference with a 95% confidence interval, or a paired t-test across the 10 seeds.
  • Report every scenario, including the ones where the smart controller loses.

Visual simulation and technology

Recommendation: Python 3 + Pygame for the visual mode, the same code headless for experiments, and Matplotlib for graphs. A single language keeps the simulation and the analysis identical, and a headless run of all scenarios (11 scenarios × 3 controllers × 10 seeds) takes minutes. Pygame can save each frame, and ffmpeg turns the frames into the video.

The screen should show: the intersection drawn to scale with both lanes per approach, cars coloured by movement, 8 signal heads (G/Y/R) at the stop lines, 16 sensor markers that light up when occupied, a live panel with simulation time, scenario, controller name (Smart / FT-A / FT-B), current target group, each movement's waiting time and priority, a small queue-length bar per lane, and a speed control (1×, 5×, 20×). A side-by-side mode (fixed on the left, smart on the right, same arrivals) makes the most convincing video.

Alternatives: a browser JavaScript canvas is easy to share but duplicates the analysis code. SUMO with TraCI is the research-grade microsimulator and would add credibility, but its learning curve is steep for a course project. Mention it as future work.

Hardware link: the two real IR sensors can drive the virtual NS D1 and D2 through serial (Arduino/ESP32 → Python) in a “hardware-in-the-loop” demo mode. The same controller code then reacts to a hand-moved model car.

15Weaknesses and limitations

  • Two point sensors carry little information. The controller cannot tell 5 cars from 20. Ablation G12 (perfect-queue oracle) measures what this costs. Report it rather than hiding it.
  • Oversaturation. When demand exceeds capacity, actuated control degenerates to max-outs (S7b). Fixed max greens then act like a poorly tuned fixed plan.
  • Dilemma-zone protection is not modelled. Real 50 km/h approaches often use advance detectors farther upstream. Our D2 serves queue estimation, not safe termination.
  • Simplified driving. Discharge follows a headway rule, not car-following. There is no lane changing, no red-light running, no heavy vehicles and no driver variability beyond random arrivals.
  • Left-lane storage is unlimited. Real short turn bays can overflow and block the straight lane. The smart controller's advantage there is unknown.
  • Merges treated as conflicts. This is conservative. Some agencies would allow, for example, NS with EL when the receiving lanes are separate. That would change the group structure.
  • No pedestrians. Pedestrian clearance would raise minimum greens and reduce the flexibility of short, single-signal greens.
  • Isolated intersection only. In a corridor, fixed-time coordination can beat local actuation. That comparison is out of scope.
  • Perfect sensors by default. IR sensors outdoors suffer sunlight interference and missed detections. Hence the robustness runs.
  • FT-B is an oracle. It knows the demand in advance. Losing to it in steady scenarios is expected and not a failure of the smart design.
  • Parameter tuning risk. τ, Q̂ and W_MAX are our choices. Freeze them before testing (§10) and publish the sensitivity runs.

16Implementation plan

  1. Conflict model and invariant. Code conflicts.py and unit-test all 64 matrix entries against §3.
  2. Signal state machine. Implement G→Y→AR→R with fixed durations. Test that yellow never goes back to green and that a head cannot turn green while any conflicting head is non-red.
  3. Traffic generator. Implement the scenario definitions (§10) as data (JSON/YAML). Write arrival CSVs per seed, and check the generated hourly counts against the target volumes.
  4. Vehicle and queue model. Implement the discharge rule and the yellow rule. Test that a 10-car queue clears in 21.0 s.
  5. Fixed controller (FT-A). Run S1 headless and validate delay against the Webster estimates (≈ 35 s straight, 58 s left). Only continue once this passes.
  6. Sensor model. Implement point occupancy. Test level-1/level-2 detection with scripted queues of 3 and 6 cars, and test that a passing car does not latch level 2.
  7. Smart controller. Implement §7 and replay the brief's SS/SL → SS/NS example as a scripted unit test. It should reproduce the timeline in §7.
  8. Statistics. Record per-vehicle arrival, stop-line crossing and free-flow crossing times, plus 1 s queue samples and signal-change logs.
  9. Tuning freeze. Adjust only on S1 with seeds 101–105, then lock the parameters.
  10. Experiments. Run all scenarios × {FT-A, FT-B, Smart} × seeds 1–10, plus ablations and the demand sweep. Save everything to CSV.
  11. Graphs. Generate G1–G8 (and the optional graphs) from the CSVs with one script, so they can be regenerated.
  12. Visualization and media. Build the Pygame view, capture screenshots (S1 partial transition, S3 single-signal green, S5 burst, S10 starvation hold) and record a side-by-side video of S6.
  13. Hardware demo. Map the 2 IR sensors to the NS D1/D2 inputs over serial.
  14. Report. Sections follow this document. Results report means ± 95% CI and include the losses.

The fixed-time green values in §8 came from a short Webster calculation using the parameters above. Reproduce them in your own code as step 5's first test.
